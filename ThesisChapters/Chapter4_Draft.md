## 4.1 Introduction

This chapter presented the results of the experimental evaluation described in Chapter 3,
comparing software-based ASCON-128 with hardware-accelerated AES-128-GCM on the
ESP32-WROOM-32E across the full Sleep → Wake → Sense → Encrypt → Transmit → Sleep duty
cycle. The chapter was structured to answer, in order, the three objectives set out in
§1.8: the total duty-cycle energy and per-byte energy efficiency of each algorithm,
including the hardware engine's initialization overhead (Objective 1); the energy cost per
transmitted byte across the full duty cycle (Objective 2); and the conditions, including
the break-even payload size, under which software-based ASCON-128 remained preferable to
hardware-accelerated AES-128-GCM (Objective 3).

The chapter began with a summary of how the raw benchmark logs were converted into the
cleaned datasets that all subsequent statistics were computed from (§4.2), since the
reliability of every downstream result depended on that process. The remaining sections
presented results by metric family: execution time and throughput (§4.3), power
consumption (§4.4), per-byte energy and the break-even analysis (§4.5), memory footprint
(§4.6), and the statistical significance tests that supported the findings (§4.7). §4.8
closed the chapter with a summary organized around the three objectives, setting up the
interpretation carried out in Chapter 5.

Both the ASCON-128 and AES-128-GCM datasets were processed through an identical pipeline —
the same iteration-completeness filter, the same two sensor-calibration constants, and the
same outlier-trimming procedure (§4.2) — so that any difference reported between the two
algorithms in this chapter could be attributed to the algorithms themselves rather than to
inconsistent data handling.

## 4.2 Data Collection Outcome and Cleaning Summary

### 4.2.1 Raw Log Volumes and Structural Completeness

The firmware described in §3.3 produced one newline-delimited JSON record per encryption
trial, logged during the `ENCRYPT_AND_MEASURE` phase of each duty cycle. The raw log for
ASCON-128 contained 65,506 records and the raw log for AES-128-GCM contained 61,715
records; no malformed or truncated lines were encountered in either log during parsing.

Rather than de-duplicating these records by the trial `id` field, the logs were segmented
into complete outer-loop iterations, since `id` was not guaranteed to increment
monotonically across device resets — an `id` collision could span two genuinely different
sweeps rather than indicate a retransmitted reading. An iteration boundary was instead
detected from the payload-size sequence itself: because each sweep progressed through
payload sizes from 16 bytes to 1,600 bytes in fixed 16-byte steps (matching `Size = 16 × j`
from the payload generation algorithm in §3.3.2), a drop in the recorded size back to 16
bytes marked the start of a new sweep. This segmentation was performed on the log in its
original recorded order.

This overall structure — an outer loop of repeated sweeps and an inner loop of 100
payload sizes stepping from 16 to 1,600 bytes in fixed 16-byte increments, with each
message generated by a pseudo-random number generator seeded with a constant value
of 128 — followed the benchmarking design established for the ESP32 platform by
[Jin & Becker, 2022] (§3.3.2), whose own evaluation used an identical outer-loop/inner-loop
structure (500 repeats × 100 payload sizes) but was limited to execution time and
throughput. This study extended that design with the power, memory, and full-duty-cycle
instrumentation absent from the original benchmarking approach, detailed in §4.2.2–§4.2.4
below.

This process identified 699 candidate iterations in the ASCON-128 log and 646 candidate
iterations in the AES-128-GCM log — more than the 500 outer-loop repeats specified in
§3.3.1, since a number of these candidate sweeps were partial fragments left by device
resets or reboots rather than genuine additional runs. Only iterations containing all 100
distinct payload sizes exactly once were retained; partial sweeps were discarded outright
rather than repaired or interpolated. This yielded 561 complete iterations for ASCON-128
and 510 complete iterations for AES-128-GCM, each contributing exactly one reading per
payload size — that is, 561 clean samples for every one of the 100 ASCON-128 payload
levels, and 510 for every AES-128-GCM payload level, with zero variance in the per-size
sample count for either algorithm. Because both complete-iteration counts exceeded the
pre-registered 500-repeat design, all complete iterations were retained for analysis rather
than subsampled to a fixed count, in order to maximize statistical power; this decision was
applied identically to both algorithms.

### 4.2.2 INA219 Zero-Offset Calibration

Inspection of the iteration-complete data showed that approximately 21% of `current_before`
and `current_after` readings were negative. These negative values did not resemble
scattered measurement noise: the middle 50% of negative readings clustered tightly between
approximately −6.5 mA and −5.2 mA, with a small number of much larger negative spikes (down
to roughly −76 mA and −99 mA) occurring separately as genuine transient noise rather than
part of the cluster. This tight, repeatable clustering was consistent with a sensor
zero-offset error, in which the INA219's calibrated "zero current" reading sits a constant
amount below the true physical zero, shifting every reading — including physically valid
positive ones — down by the same fixed amount.

Rather than floor or clip these readings at zero, which would discard the offset signal and
bias every low-current reading by an inconsistent amount depending on its distance from
zero, the offset was estimated directly from the data and corrected. The offset was
calculated as the mean of the absolute median negative `current_before` and
`current_after` values across the ASCON-128 iteration-complete data, giving a shared
calibration constant of **5.70 mA**. This constant was added back to every current reading
in both datasets — not only the negative ones — and the small number of readings that
remained negative after correction (a genuine noise residual, not part of the offset) were
clipped at zero. Power was then recomputed from the corrected current using an empirically
measured bus voltage, rather than the device's own raw power fields, which had inherited
the same sensor bias. The bus voltage was estimated as the median of `power ÷ current` on
clearly-active, high-current rows, giving **4.674 V** — lower than the nominal 5 V supply
rail, consistent with the voltage drop across the INA219's internal shunt resistor.

Both constants — the 5.70 mA offset and the 4.674 V measured bus voltage — were estimated
once, from the ASCON-128 dataset, and applied identically to the AES-128-GCM dataset. This
ensured that the calibration step itself could not introduce a systematic difference
between the two algorithms' reported power figures.

### 4.2.3 Outlier Trimming

After the structural (§4.2.1) and calibration (§4.2.2) corrections were applied, IQR-based
trimming (k = 1.5) was applied to execution time and the corrected power delta, grouped by
payload size. This ordering was deliberate: applying outlier trimming before the
iteration-completeness filter or the calibration correction would have risked treating
structural artifacts (partial sweeps) or systematic sensor bias as if they were statistical
noise, rather than correcting them at their source.

This step removed 10,355 rows from the ASCON-128 dataset (56,100 → 45,745 rows) and 8,130
rows from the AES-128-GCM dataset (51,000 → 42,870 rows). No single payload size lost a
disproportionate share of its rows in either dataset, indicating that the trimming targeted
genuine per-trial noise rather than a systematic effect concentrated at particular payload
sizes.

### 4.2.4 Sleep-Phase Energy Constant

The firmware instrumented only the active `ENCRYPT_AND_MEASURE` phase of the duty cycle;
the deep-sleep phase was not measured on-device. Sleep-phase energy was instead added
analytically, following §3.3.4's specification of a 60-second deep-sleep interval, using
the relation:

```
E_sleep = V_sleep × I_sleep × T_sleep
```

The deep-sleep current figure was taken from the Espressif *ESP32-C3 Series Datasheet,
Version 2.3*, Table 5-9 ("Current Consumption in Low-Power Modes"), which specifies a
typical deep-sleep current of **5 µA** for the RTC timer + RTC memory wake configuration
used in this study, measured at a 3.3 V supply and 25 °C ambient temperature (§5.6.1 of
that datasheet). Using `V_sleep = 3.3 V`, `I_sleep = 5 µA`, and the design-specified
`T_sleep = 60 s` gave:

```
E_sleep = 3.3 V × 5 µA × 60 s = 990 µJ
```

This value was added to every payload size's active-phase energy figure to produce the
total duty-cycle energy reported in §4.5. It should be noted that, during the actual data
collection runs, the firmware's `DEEP_SLEEP_SECONDS` constant was temporarily set to 1
second rather than the 60-second design value, purely to shorten the total collection time
required to gather the iteration counts reported in §4.2.1. Because the raw log contains
only active-phase measurements and `E_sleep` above was computed analytically from the
datasheet current and the fixed 60-second design duration — independent of the board's
actual sleep-timer setting during collection — this deviation had no effect on any reported
metric. The reasoning behind why this substitution does not threaten the validity of the
total-energy figures is developed further in §5.5.

## 4.3 Execution Time and Throughput Results

### 4.3.1 Execution Time Scaling

Mean execution time was computed for each of the 100 payload sizes, for both algorithms,
from the cleaned trial-level data (§4.2.3), together with the median, standard deviation,
and 95% confidence interval (using the t-distribution). At the smallest payload size (16
bytes), ASCON-128 completed a trial in a mean of 1,370.9 µs (95% CI: 1,370.7–1,371.0 µs,
n = 540), while AES-128-GCM required 1,740.8 µs (95% CI: 1,740.7–1,741.0 µs, n = 482). At
the largest payload size (1,600 bytes), ASCON-128's mean execution time had risen to
3,266.5 µs, while AES-128-GCM's had risen to 3,167.6 µs — marginally lower than ASCON-128's
at this payload size, despite AES-128-GCM being slower at every smaller payload size
tested. Figure 4.1 presents the full execution-time curve for both algorithms, with the
95% confidence interval shown as a shaded band around each mean.

**Figure 4.1** *(`figures/execution_time_scaling.png`)* — Mean execution time (µs) versus
payload size (16–1,600 bytes) for ASCON-128 and AES-128-GCM, with 95% confidence interval
bands.

### 4.3.2 Throughput

Throughput was computed per Equation 1 (§3.5.2), using the grouped mean execution time for
each payload size rather than a per-trial calculation. Both algorithms' throughput
increased with payload size, reflecting the fixed per-operation overhead (nonce generation,
function-call and measurement overhead) being amortized across a larger number of
encrypted bytes as payload size grew. At 1,600 bytes, ASCON-128 achieved a throughput of
approximately 489,815 bytes/second, while AES-128-GCM achieved approximately 505,113
bytes/second — consistent with AES-128-GCM's shorter execution time at this payload size.

**Figure 4.2** *(`figures/throughput_scaling.png`)* — Throughput (bytes/second) versus
payload size for ASCON-128 and AES-128-GCM.

### 4.3.3 Linear Scaling and Regression Fit

To evaluate whether execution time scaled linearly with payload size, a linear regression
of mean execution time against payload size was performed for each algorithm. Both
algorithms exhibited near-perfect linear scaling: ASCON-128 produced a slope of 1.197
µs/byte with R² = 0.9999997 (essentially 1.0), and AES-128-GCM produced a slope of 0.901
µs/byte with R² = 0.9999998. The lower slope for AES-128-GCM indicated that, once its
larger fixed per-operation cost was accounted for, the hardware-accelerated algorithm added
less additional time per byte of payload than ASCON-128's software implementation — this is
the numerical basis for the execution-time crossover visible in Figure 4.1, where
AES-128-GCM's higher fixed cost was progressively offset by its lower marginal cost per
byte as payload size increased. This near-perfect linear scaling for ASCON-128 is discussed
further in relation to the sponge-duplexing scaling behaviour described in §2.2.5.

## 4.4 Power Consumption Results

Mean corrected power (§4.2.2) was computed per payload size for both algorithms. At the
smallest payload size (16 bytes), ASCON-128 drew a mean power delta of 20.3 mW while
AES-128-GCM drew 24.2 mW. At 800 bytes, this gap widened considerably: ASCON-128 drew 52.2
mW while AES-128-GCM drew only 22.2 mW — a reversal from the smallest payload size, at
which AES-128-GCM had drawn more power. At the largest payload size (1,600 bytes),
ASCON-128 drew 52.8 mW against AES-128-GCM's 48.1 mW.

This pattern indicated that ASCON-128's power draw scaled upward more steeply with payload
size than AES-128-GCM's, consistent with ASCON-128's operations remaining entirely
CPU-bound throughout the sponge permutation, while AES-128-GCM's hardware engine absorbed a
larger share of the computational load into dedicated silicon rather than the CPU as
payload size increased. The consequence of this power-scaling difference for per-byte
energy efficiency, which is the metric most directly relevant to Objectives 1 and 2, is
presented in §4.5.

## 4.5 Per-Byte Energy Efficiency and Break-Even Analysis

### 4.5.1 Per-Byte Energy Curves

Per-operation energy was computed as the product of mean power (mW) and mean execution
time (ms) for each payload size, and per-byte energy was computed by dividing this figure
by the payload size in bytes. At the smallest payload size (16 bytes), ASCON-128 consumed
1.742 µJ/byte of active-phase energy, compared with AES-128-GCM's 2.633 µJ/byte —
ASCON-128 was markedly more efficient at this payload size. This gap narrowed rapidly as
payload size increased: by 336 bytes, ASCON-128 consumed 0.107 µJ/byte against
AES-128-GCM's 0.150 µJ/byte, and by 352 bytes the ordering had reversed, with ASCON-128 at
0.106 µJ/byte against AES-128-GCM's now-lower 0.086 µJ/byte. At the largest payload size
tested (1,600 bytes), ASCON-128 consumed 0.108 µJ/byte compared with AES-128-GCM's 0.095
µJ/byte.

Adding the sleep-phase energy constant from §4.2.4 (990 µJ, identical for both algorithms
at every payload size) produced the total duty-cycle energy per byte — the figure most
directly relevant to Objectives 1 and 2, since it captured the complete
Sleep → Wake → Sense → Encrypt → Transmit → Sleep cycle rather than the active encryption
phase alone. Because the sleep-phase term was constant across payload size, it shifted the
absolute level of both curves upward without altering their shape or the location of the
break-even point identified in §4.5.2: at 16 bytes, ASCON-128's total duty-cycle energy was
63.62 µJ/byte against AES-128-GCM's 64.51 µJ/byte; at 1,600 bytes, ASCON-128's total figure
had fallen to 0.727 µJ/byte against AES-128-GCM's 0.714 µJ/byte.

**Figure 4.4** *(`figures/energy_per_byte_comparison.png`)* — Active-phase energy per byte
(µJ/byte) versus payload size for ASCON-128 and AES-128-GCM, with the break-even payload
size marked.

**Figure 4.5** *(`figures/total_duty_cycle_energy_per_byte.png`)* — Total duty-cycle energy
per byte (µJ/byte, including the analytically-derived sleep-phase term) versus payload

### 4.5.2 Break-Even / Crossover Payload Size

The break-even payload size was identified by computing, for each of the 100 payload
sizes, the difference `ascon_minus_aes_energy = energy_per_byte_uJ(ASCON) −
energy_per_byte_uJ(AES)`, and locating the sign change in this difference across ascending
payload size. A clean sign change was identified between the 336-byte payload size
(difference = −0.043 µJ/byte, ASCON-128 more efficient) and the 352-byte payload size
(difference = +0.020 µJ/byte, AES-128-GCM more efficient). Linear interpolation between
these two bracketing sizes gave an interpolated break-even payload size of **approximately
347 bytes**. This crossover point was identical whether computed from active-phase energy
or from total duty-cycle energy (including the sleep-phase term), since the sleep-phase
term was a constant added identically to both curves and therefore could not move a sign
change already present in the active-phase difference.

A second, much smaller sign change was detected between 528 and 560 bytes, where the
difference between the two curves oscillated within a band of approximately ±0.003 µJ/byte
— less than 3% of the magnitude of the 336–352 byte crossing. This was interpreted as the
two curves converging to near-parity in that region rather than as a second, genuine
efficiency-regime change, and is reported here as such rather than as an additional
break-even point.

### 4.5.3 Regime Segmentation

To relate the break-even result directly to realistic payload regimes, the comparison
dataset was segmented into a small-payload regime (< 256 bytes, representative of
narrow-band LPWAN-style traffic) and a large-payload regime (> 800 bytes, representative of
bulk-telemetry traffic). In the small-payload regime, ASCON-128 was more efficient on
average, with a mean difference of −0.225 µJ/byte (ASCON-128 lower). In the large-payload
regime, AES-128-GCM was more efficient on average, with a mean difference of +0.065
µJ/byte (AES-128-GCM lower). This provided the direct, data-based answer to Objective 3's
requirement to identify the conditions under which software-based ASCON-128 remained
preferable to hardware-accelerated AES-128-GCM.

*(source: `processed/regime_segmentation.json`)*

| Large (> 800 B, bulk-telemetry-typical) | +0.065 µJ/byte | AES-128-GCM |

## 4.6 Memory Footprint Results

Total memory footprint was calculated per trial as the sum of peak heap usage and stack
usage, and averaged across all payload sizes for each algorithm. ASCON-128 recorded a mean
memory footprint of 2,772.8 bytes, while AES-128-GCM recorded a mean memory footprint of
3,441.0 bytes — approximately 668 bytes, or 24%, higher than ASCON-128. This difference
was broadly constant across payload sizes rather than scaling with payload size (2,771.0
bytes at 16 bytes payload versus 2,767.6 bytes at 1,600 bytes payload for ASCON-128; 3,463.2
bytes versus 3,449.5 bytes for AES-128-GCM over the same range), consistent with the
memory-footprint methodology described in §3.5.4 and supporting its treatment as a discrete,
largely size-independent comparison rather than a scaling relationship. This is consistent
with AES-128-GCM's use of the mbedTLS library's cryptographic context structures (§2.3.1),
which carry a fixed memory overhead independent of the amount of data being encrypted.

## 4.7 Statistical Significance Testing

### 4.7.1 Per-Size Hypothesis Testing

Following §3.7.2, one-tailed independent (Welch's) t-tests were conducted at a significance
level of α = 0.01, run separately for each of the 100 payload sizes, for execution time,
power, and energy per byte. Rather than reporting a single pooled significance figure, the
proportion of payload sizes at which the difference between algorithms was statistically
significant was reported for each metric, consistent with the 100-payload-level scaling
design described in §3.3.2.

Execution time differed significantly between the two algorithms at only 21 of the 100
payload sizes tested (21%). Power differed significantly at 78 of the 100 payload sizes
(78%), and energy per byte differed significantly at 72 of the 100 payload sizes (72%).
This pattern indicated that, while the two algorithms' execution times converged closely
enough across much of the payload range to be statistically indistinguishable at most
sizes (consistent with the near-equal regression slopes reported in §4.3.3), their power
draw and resulting energy efficiency differed significantly at the large majority of
payload sizes tested — indicating that the energy-efficiency differences reported in §4.5
were driven predominantly by differences in power consumption rather than by differences in
raw execution speed.

### 4.7.2 Regression Summary

Table 4.2 (§4.3.3) is repeated here in the context of Objective 1's requirement to account
for the hardware engine's initialization overhead. The linear regression of per-operation
energy against payload size produced an intercept of 15.03 µJ for ASCON-128 and 30.14 µJ
for AES-128-GCM — that is, extrapolating each algorithm's energy-scaling line back to a
hypothetical zero-byte payload, AES-128-GCM carried a fixed per-operation energy cost
approximately double that of ASCON-128. Because this fixed cost was independent of payload
size, it was interpreted as evidence of the AES hardware engine's initialization and
setup overhead — the specific quantity Objective 1 required this evaluation to isolate —
and is treated as such in the discussion of Objective 1 in §5.2.1.

## 4.8 Summary of Chapter 4

This chapter reported the results of a controlled, energy-centric comparison between
software-based ASCON-128 and hardware-accelerated AES-128-GCM across a full IoT duty
cycle, following the identical cleaning, calibration, and statistical pipeline described in
§4.2 for both algorithms.

With respect to **Objective 1** (total duty-cycle energy and per-byte energy efficiency,
including hardware engine initialization overhead), execution time scaled almost perfectly
linearly with payload size for both algorithms (§4.3.3), and AES-128-GCM's energy
regression carried a fixed per-operation cost roughly double that of ASCON-128's (§4.7.2)
— the numerical signature of the hardware engine's initialization overhead. AES-128-GCM
also used approximately 24% more memory on average (§4.6).

With respect to **Objective 2** (energy cost per transmitted byte across the full duty
cycle), both the active-phase and total duty-cycle per-byte energy figures were reported
across the full 16–1,600 byte payload range (§4.5.1), with the sleep-phase contribution
(990 µJ, from the cited datasheet figure) added identically to both algorithms.

With respect to **Objective 3** (conditions under which ASCON-128 remained preferable,
including the break-even payload size), a break-even payload size of approximately 347
bytes was identified (§4.5.2): ASCON-128 was the more energy-efficient choice below this
size, and AES-128-GCM became more energy-efficient above it, a result confirmed by the
regime segmentation into small- and large-payload categories (§4.5.3).

The statistical tests reported in §4.7.1 indicated that these energy and power differences
were significant at the majority of payload sizes tested, while execution-time differences
were significant at a much smaller minority — supporting the interpretation that the
energy-efficiency comparison presented in this chapter was driven primarily by differences
in power consumption between the two implementations, rather than by differences in raw
computational speed. The interpretation of these findings against the research objectives
and the existing literature, together with a discussion of the reliability and validity of
the methodology used to produce them, is presented in Chapter 5.
